Hypothyroidism
Thyroid
⚙️ Physiology & Pathophysiology
The thyroid gland produces insufficient T3 and T4. Most common cause: Hashimoto's thyroiditis (autoimmune destruction of thyroid tissue — anti-TPO antibodies). Other causes: post-radioiodine/surgical ablation, iodine deficiency, and secondary/tertiary causes (pituitary or hypothalamic failure). Low T3/T4 removes negative feedback on the pituitary → TSH rises (primary). Reduced thyroid hormone slows cellular metabolism affecting virtually every organ system.
🩺 Clinical Features
  • Fatigue & lethargy
  • Weight gain
  • Cold intolerance
  • Dry skin & hair, hair loss
  • Constipation
  • Bradycardia
  • Menstrual irregularity
  • Myxoedema (periorbital oedema, hoarse voice)
  • Depression, cognitive slowing
  • Delayed tendon reflexes
  • Goitre (Hashimoto's)
🧪 Investigations
  • TSH ↑ (primary), TSH ↓ (secondary)
  • Free T4 ↓
  • Anti-TPO antibodies (Hashimoto's)
  • Anti-thyroglobulin antibodies
  • FBC (normocytic anaemia)
  • Lipid panel (hypercholesterolaemia)
  • ECG (low voltage, bradycardia)
  • Thyroid ultrasound
💊 Treatment
  • Levothyroxine (T4 replacement) — titrated by TSH level
  • Start low dose in elderly or cardiac disease patients
  • Monitor TSH at 6 weeks after starting then annually
  • Hashimoto's: autoimmune condition — no cure, manage with replacement
  • Myxoedema coma: IV T3/T4 + hydrocortisone + ICU admission

Hyperthyroidism
Thyroid
⚙️ Physiology & Pathophysiology
Excess T3/T4 production due to: Graves' disease (TSI/TRAb autoantibodies stimulate the TSH receptor — most common cause), toxic multinodular goitre, toxic adenoma, or subacute thyroiditis (release of pre-formed stored hormone). High T3/T4 suppresses TSH. Results in increased adrenergic sensitivity, accelerated metabolism, and a hyperdynamic cardiovascular state.
🩺 Clinical Features
  • Heat intolerance, sweating
  • Weight loss despite increased appetite
  • Tachycardia, palpitations, atrial fibrillation
  • Fine tremor (hands)
  • Anxiety, irritability, insomnia
  • Diarrhoea
  • Goitre (diffuse in Graves')
  • Exophthalmos/proptosis (Graves' only)
  • Pretibial myxoedema (Graves' only)
  • Proximal myopathy
  • Oligomenorrhoea
🧪 Investigations
  • TSH ↓ (often undetectable)
  • Free T3 and T4 ↑
  • TRAb/TSI (Graves' — positive ~95%)
  • Anti-TPO (may be positive)
  • Thyroid uptake scan: diffuse = Graves'; hot nodule = toxic adenoma; low = thyroiditis
  • ECG (AF, sinus tachycardia)
  • FBC, LFTs
💊 Treatment
  • Antithyroids: carbimazole or PTU (block synthesis)
  • Beta-blockers: propranolol for symptomatic control (tremor, HR)
  • Radioiodine (I-131): definitive treatment — contraindicated in pregnancy
  • Thyroidectomy: for large goitre, pressure symptoms, or malignancy concern
  • Graves' ophthalmopathy: steroids, selenium, orbital decompression
  • Thyroid storm: PTU + potassium iodide + dexamethasone + propranolol + ICU

Hyperparathyroidism
Parathyroid
⚙️ Physiology & Pathophysiology
Primary: autonomous PTH overproduction — parathyroid adenoma (85%), hyperplasia (14%), carcinoma (1%). PTH acts on bone (↑ osteoclast resorption → Ca²⁺ release), kidney (↑ Ca²⁺ reabsorption, ↓ phosphate reabsorption, ↑ vitamin D activation), and gut (↑ Ca²⁺ absorption via vitamin D). Net result: hypercalcaemia + hypophosphataemia. Secondary: reactive PTH ↑ due to hypocalcaemia (CKD, vitamin D deficiency). Tertiary: autonomous gland after prolonged secondary stimulus (e.g. long-standing CKD).
🩺 Clinical Features — "Bones, Stones, Groans, Moans"
  • Bones: bone pain, osteoporosis, fractures
  • Stones: renal calculi (calcium oxalate/phosphate)
  • Groans: nausea, vomiting, constipation, anorexia
  • Moans: depression, cognitive impairment, fatigue
  • Polyuria/polydipsia (nephrogenic DI from hypercalcaemia)
  • Often asymptomatic — incidental finding on bloods
🧪 Investigations
  • Serum Ca²⁺ ↑ (corrected or ionised)
  • PTH ↑ (primary/tertiary)
  • Phosphate ↓ (primary)
  • 24h urinary calcium ↑
  • ALP ↑ (bone involvement)
  • Vitamin D levels
  • Sestamibi parathyroid scan (localise adenoma)
  • DEXA scan (bone density)
  • Renal ultrasound (calculi)
💊 Treatment
  • Asymptomatic primary: monitor (calcium, renal function, DEXA)
  • Surgery (parathyroidectomy): symptomatic, Ca > 2.85 mmol/L, young age, osteoporosis, renal stones
  • Cinacalcet (calcimimetic): for non-surgical candidates
  • Bisphosphonates: bone protection
  • Hypercalcaemic crisis: IV saline + furosemide + IV bisphosphonates
  • Secondary: correct underlying cause + vitamin D/calcium supplementation

Hypoparathyroidism
Parathyroid
⚙️ Physiology & Pathophysiology
Deficient PTH → decreased bone resorption, decreased renal Ca²⁺ reabsorption, decreased active vitamin D production → hypocalcaemia + hyperphosphataemia. Most common cause: inadvertent removal or devascularisation of parathyroid glands during thyroid or neck surgery. Other causes: autoimmune, DiGeorge syndrome (22q11.2 deletion — parathyroid aplasia), hypomagnesaemia (magnesium required for PTH secretion).
🩺 Clinical Features
  • Perioral numbness, tingling fingers/toes
  • Tetany: carpopedal spasm
  • Chvostek's sign (facial nerve tap → facial twitch)
  • Trousseau's sign (BP cuff inflated → carpal spasm)
  • Seizures (severe hypocalcaemia)
  • Prolonged QT → arrhythmia
  • Anxiety, depression
  • Cataracts (chronic)
  • Dry skin, brittle nails, hair loss
🧪 Investigations
  • Serum Ca²⁺ ↓
  • PTH ↓ (diagnostic)
  • Phosphate ↑
  • Magnesium levels
  • ECG (prolonged QT interval)
  • Vitamin D levels
  • Urinary calcium (low)
💊 Treatment
  • Acute severe: IV calcium gluconate (slow IV push — cardiac monitoring)
  • Chronic: oral calcium supplements + calcitriol (active vitamin D — bypasses need for PTH)
  • Correct hypomagnesaemia if present
  • Recombinant PTH (teriparatide): newer option for chronic disease
  • Monitor calcium regularly — avoid over-correction

Type 1 Diabetes
Diabetes
⚙️ Physiology & Pathophysiology
Autoimmune destruction of pancreatic β-cells (islets of Langerhans), mediated by T-lymphocytes and autoantibodies (anti-GAD, anti-islet cell, anti-insulin). Results in absolute insulin deficiency. Without insulin: cells cannot uptake glucose → hyperglycaemia; unregulated fat breakdown → free fatty acids → ketogenesis → diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA). Glucagon is unopposed. Typically presents in childhood or adolescence but can occur at any age.
🩺 Clinical Features
  • Polyuria (osmotic diuresis)
  • Polydipsia (fluid loss compensation)
  • Polyphagia with weight loss
  • Fatigue
  • DKA: nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain
  • Kussmaul breathing (deep, rapid — compensatory)
  • Fruity/ketone breath
  • Altered consciousness (severe DKA)
  • Recurrent infections
  • Blurred vision (osmotic lens changes)
🧪 Investigations
  • Fasting glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L or random ≥ 11.1
  • HbA1c ≥ 48 mmol/mol (6.5%)
  • C-peptide ↓/undetectable (absent β-cell function)
  • Anti-GAD, anti-islet cell, anti-insulin antibodies (positive)
  • DKA: ABG (pH < 7.35, HCO₃ low, anion gap ↑), ketones ↑
  • Urinalysis: glycosuria, ketonuria
  • Annual: HbA1c, lipids, renal function, urine ACR, fundoscopy, foot check
💊 Treatment
  • Lifelong insulin therapy — no oral alternative
  • Multiple Daily Injections (MDI): basal + bolus regimen
  • Insulin pump (CSII): continuous subcutaneous infusion
  • Continuous Glucose Monitoring (CGM)
  • Carbohydrate counting and sick day rules
  • DKA management: IV fluids, insulin infusion, potassium replacement
  • Target HbA1c < 53 mmol/mol (7%)
  • Annual screening: nephropathy, retinopathy, neuropathy, cardiovascular

Type 2 Diabetes
Diabetes
⚙️ Physiology & Pathophysiology
Combination of peripheral insulin resistance (muscle, liver, adipose tissue) and progressive β-cell dysfunction leading to relative insulin deficiency. Driven by obesity, physical inactivity, and genetic predisposition. Insulin resistance → compensatory hyperinsulinaemia → eventual β-cell exhaustion → hyperglycaemia. Strongly associated with dyslipidaemia and hypertension (metabolic syndrome). Insidious onset — often discovered incidentally on routine blood tests.
🩺 Clinical Features
  • Often asymptomatic for many years
  • Polyuria, polydipsia, fatigue (when significantly hyperglycaemic)
  • Recurrent infections (UTI, skin, candida)
  • Slow wound healing
  • Blurred vision
  • Acanthosis nigricans (insulin resistance marker)
  • Peripheral neuropathy (tingling/numbness feet)
  • HHS in severe uncontrolled hyperglycaemia
🧪 Investigations
  • Fasting glucose ≥ 7.0 or 2h OGTT ≥ 11.1 mmol/L
  • HbA1c ≥ 48 mmol/mol (6.5%)
  • C-peptide: present (reduced in late disease)
  • Lipid panel (dyslipidaemia)
  • Urine albumin:creatinine ratio (microalbuminuria)
  • eGFR (renal function)
  • Annual dilated fundoscopy
  • Annual foot examination
💊 Treatment
  • Lifestyle: weight loss, diet modification, exercise (first-line)
  • Metformin (first-line oral agent)
  • SGLT2 inhibitors (cardio/renal protective): empagliflozin, dapagliflozin
  • GLP-1 agonists (weight loss + CV benefit): semaglutide, dulaglutide
  • DPP-4 inhibitors: saxagliptin, sitagliptin
  • Sulfonylureas: glipizide, glibenclamide
  • Insulin when oral agents insufficient
  • Target HbA1c 53–58 mmol/mol (7–7.5%)
  • CV risk management: statin, antihypertensives, antiplatelet therapy

Diabetes Insipidus
Diabetes
⚙️ Physiology & Pathophysiology
Central DI: deficiency of ADH (vasopressin) from the posterior pituitary due to trauma, surgery, tumours (craniopharyngioma), inflammation, or idiopathic causes. Nephrogenic DI: renal tubule resistance to ADH (lithium toxicity, hypercalcaemia, CKD, genetic AVPR2/AQP2 mutations). Without ADH action: collecting duct remains impermeable to water → massive dilute urine output (up to 20L/day) → hypernatraemia and dehydration if fluid intake cannot compensate.
🩺 Clinical Features
  • Polyuria (large volume, very dilute urine — USG < 1.005)
  • Intense polydipsia
  • Nocturia
  • Dehydration if fluid access restricted
  • Hypernatraemia: irritability, confusion, weakness
  • Growth failure and hyperthermia (children)
  • Enuresis in children
🧪 Investigations
  • Urine osmolality ↓ (< 300 mOsm/kg)
  • Serum osmolality ↑ (> 295)
  • Serum Na⁺ ↑ (hypernatraemia)
  • Serum ADH levels (low in central DI)
  • Water deprivation test: failure to concentrate urine confirms DI
  • DDAVP challenge: response = central DI; no response = nephrogenic
  • MRI brain: absent posterior pituitary bright spot (central DI)
💊 Treatment
  • Central DI: DDAVP (desmopressin) — intranasal, oral, or subcutaneous
  • Nephrogenic DI: treat underlying cause (stop lithium, correct hypercalcaemia)
  • Thiazide diuretics paradoxically reduce urine output in nephrogenic DI
  • Low salt/low protein diet (reduces renal solute load)
  • Adequate hydration is crucial in all forms
  • Indomethacin (adjunct in nephrogenic DI)

SIADH
Pituitary / Systemic
⚙️ Physiology & Pathophysiology
Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone secretion — excess ADH activity despite low serum osmolality. Causes: CNS disease (meningitis, stroke, trauma), pulmonary disease (TB, pneumonia), ectopic ADH production (SCLC — small cell lung cancer), drugs (SSRIs, carbamazepine, cyclophosphamide, opioids), pain, nausea. ADH drives water reabsorption → dilutional hyponatraemia. Kidneys continue to excrete sodium (urine Na⁺ > 40 mmol/L) despite low serum Na⁺. Patient is euvolaemic — no oedema.
🩺 Clinical Features
  • Often asymptomatic in mild hyponatraemia
  • Nausea and vomiting
  • Headache
  • Confusion, cognitive impairment
  • Lethargy, fatigue
  • Seizures (Na⁺ < 120 mmol/L)
  • Coma (severe)
  • Muscle cramps
  • NO peripheral oedema (euvolaemic)
🧪 Investigations
  • Serum Na⁺ ↓ (hyponatraemia)
  • Serum osmolality ↓ (< 275 mOsm/kg)
  • Urine osmolality ↑ (> 100 mOsm/kg — inappropriately concentrated)
  • Urine Na⁺ ↑ (> 40 mmol/L)
  • Thyroid and adrenal function (exclude other causes)
  • Chest X-ray (pneumonia, malignancy)
  • CT brain, CT chest (SCLC)
💊 Treatment
  • Treat underlying cause (most important step)
  • Fluid restriction < 1000–1500 mL/day — mainstay of management
  • Severe symptomatic (seizures/coma): 3% hypertonic saline — controlled correction max 8–10 mmol/L/24h
  • Avoid rapid correction → osmotic demyelination syndrome
  • Vaptans (tolvaptan, conivaptan): V2 receptor antagonists — selective water excretion
  • Demeclocycline: blocks ADH action in kidney (second-line)

Acromegaly
Pituitary
⚙️ Physiology & Pathophysiology
Excess Growth Hormone (GH) secretion in adults — almost always from a GH-secreting pituitary macroadenoma. GH stimulates IGF-1 (insulin-like growth factor-1) production from the liver, which mediates most growth effects. After epiphyseal fusion → acral and soft tissue enlargement (not height increase). Pre-fusion in children → gigantism. Excess GH causes insulin resistance, organomegaly, and metabolic disturbances including hypertension and diabetes.
🩺 Clinical Features
  • Enlarged hands and feet (ring/shoe size increase)
  • Coarse facial features: prominent brow, jaw prognathism
  • Macroglossia, increased interdental spacing
  • Hyperhidrosis (excessive sweating)
  • Arthralgia, carpal tunnel syndrome
  • Hypertension, cardiomegaly, cardiac failure
  • Sleep apnoea (obstructive)
  • Diabetes mellitus (insulin resistance)
  • Headache
  • Bitemporal hemianopia (optic chiasm compression)
  • Goitre, colonic polyps
🧪 Investigations
  • IGF-1 ↑ (best screening test — stable level)
  • GH oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT): failure to suppress GH < 1 μg/L confirms diagnosis
  • MRI pituitary (macroadenoma)
  • Visual field testing (Goldmann perimetry)
  • Echo (cardiomegaly), sleep study (OSA)
  • Colonoscopy (polyps), fasting glucose/HbA1c
💊 Treatment
  • Surgery: transsphenoidal resection (first-line if feasible)
  • Somatostatin analogues: octreotide, lanreotide (medical first-line if surgery incomplete)
  • GH receptor antagonist: pegvisomant (if SSA-resistant)
  • Dopamine agonists: cabergoline (modest efficacy, adjunct)
  • Radiotherapy: adjunctive (slow onset — months to years)
  • Target: normal IGF-1, GH < 1 μg/L on OGTT
  • Treat comorbidities: HTN, DM, OSA, carpal tunnel

Cushing's Disease
Pituitary
⚙️ Physiology & Pathophysiology
Cushing's Disease (capital D) = ACTH-secreting pituitary adenoma (corticotroph adenoma) → bilateral adrenal hyperplasia → excess cortisol. Cushing's Syndrome (broader) = any cause of cortisol excess: exogenous glucocorticoids (most common overall), ectopic ACTH (SCLC), adrenal adenoma/carcinoma. Excess cortisol: gluconeogenesis ↑ (hyperglycaemia), protein catabolism (myopathy, thin skin, purple striae), fat redistribution (central obesity, buffalo hump, moon face), immunosuppression, and mineralocorticoid activity (↑ BP, hypokalaemia).
🩺 Clinical Features
  • Central obesity with thin limbs
  • Moon face (facial plethora/rounding)
  • Buffalo hump (dorsocervical fat pad)
  • Purple striae (abdominal)
  • Proximal myopathy (difficulty rising from chair)
  • Thin skin, easy bruising
  • Hypertension, hyperglycaemia/DM
  • Osteoporosis, fractures
  • Hirsutism, acne (androgen excess)
  • Amenorrhoea
  • Depression, mood changes
🧪 Investigations
  • 24h urinary free cortisol ↑
  • Late-night salivary cortisol ↑ (loss of diurnal rhythm)
  • 1mg overnight dexamethasone suppression test: failure to suppress (cortisol > 50 nmol/L)
  • Serum ACTH: ↑ (ACTH-dependent), ↓ (adrenal adenoma/exogenous)
  • High-dose dexamethasone suppression: suppresses in Cushing's Disease; not in ectopic ACTH
  • MRI pituitary (corticotroph adenoma — often small)
  • Inferior petrosal sinus sampling (IPSS): most accurate localisation
  • CT adrenals/chest (ectopic ACTH/adrenal tumour)
💊 Treatment
  • Cushing's Disease: transsphenoidal pituitary surgery (first-line)
  • Radiotherapy if surgery incomplete
  • Medical steroidogenesis inhibitors: metyrapone, ketoconazole, mifepristone
  • Bilateral adrenalectomy (risk of Nelson's syndrome — ACTH-secreting tumour expands)
  • Ectopic ACTH: treat primary tumour
  • Adrenal adenoma: laparoscopic adrenalectomy
  • Post-op: hydrocortisone replacement (suppressed HPA axis) → gradual taper

Prolactinoma
Pituitary
⚙️ Physiology & Pathophysiology
Most common pituitary adenoma. Arises from prolactin-secreting lactotroph cells. Microprolactinoma (< 10mm) more common in women; macroprolactinoma (> 10mm) more common in men (diagnosed later due to delayed symptoms). Excess prolactin inhibits GnRH pulsatility → reduced LH/FSH → hypogonadism. Causes of raised prolactin (other than prolactinoma): pregnancy, breastfeeding, hypothyroidism (TRH stimulates PRL), dopamine-blocking drugs (antipsychotics, metoclopramide), and stalk compression from any pituitary mass.
🩺 Clinical Features
  • Women: galactorrhoea (spontaneous milk production)
  • Amenorrhoea / oligomenorrhoea
  • Infertility, loss of libido
  • Men: erectile dysfunction, reduced libido
  • Infertility (reduced sperm count)
  • Galactorrhoea (less common in men)
  • Both sexes: osteoporosis (from hypogonadism)
  • Macroprolactinoma: headache, bitemporal hemianopia, hypopituitarism
🧪 Investigations
  • Serum prolactin ↑ (> 5000 mIU/L suggests prolactinoma; > 10,000 = macroprolactinoma)
  • Exclude: pregnancy (βhCG), hypothyroidism (TSH), drug history
  • MRI pituitary (size, chiasm compression)
  • Visual field testing (macroprolactinoma)
  • LH, FSH, testosterone/oestradiol (hypogonadism)
  • DEXA scan (bone density)
💊 Treatment
  • Dopamine agonists: cabergoline (first-line, once/twice weekly oral) — reduces PRL and tumour size
  • Bromocriptine (alternative — more side effects)
  • Microprolactinoma: medical treatment preferred; trial withdrawal after 2 years if PRL normal
  • Macroprolactinoma: DA agonist first; surgery (transsphenoidal) if resistant or intolerant
  • Radiotherapy: adjunctive
  • Pregnancy: withhold cabergoline (monitor for tumour expansion in macroprolactinoma)

Hypoadrenalism (Addison's Disease)
Adrenal
⚙️ Physiology & Pathophysiology
Primary adrenal insufficiency (Addison's disease): autoimmune destruction of adrenal cortex (70–80% in developed world) → deficiency of BOTH cortisol AND aldosterone. Other causes: TB (historically), metastases, adrenal haemorrhage (Waterhouse-Friderichsen — meningococcal), adrenoleukodystrophy. Secondary: ACTH deficiency from pituitary disease → cortisol only low (aldosterone preserved via renin-angiotensin). Tertiary: iatrogenic — sudden cessation of long-term glucocorticoids. Inadequate cortisol = impaired stress response, hypoglycaemia, hypotension.
🩺 Clinical Features
  • Fatigue, weakness, lethargy
  • Weight loss, anorexia
  • Nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea
  • Hypotension, postural dizziness
  • Hyponatraemia (↓ Na⁺)
  • Hyperkalaemia (↑ K⁺) — primary only
  • Hypoglycaemia
  • Hyperpigmentation (buccal mucosa, scars, skin creases) — primary only (↑ ACTH/MSH)
  • Loss of axillary/pubic hair (women)
  • Adrenal crisis: extreme hypotension, shock, fever, confusion — life-threatening
🧪 Investigations
  • 8am serum cortisol ↓ (< 100 nmol/L highly suggestive)
  • Short Synacthen Test: 250μg ACTH given; cortisol < 500 nmol/L at 30–60 min = impaired response (gold standard)
  • ACTH ↑ (primary), ↓ (secondary)
  • Renin ↑, aldosterone ↓ (primary)
  • Electrolytes: Na⁺ ↓, K⁺ ↑ (primary)
  • Anti-adrenal antibodies (21-hydroxylase) — autoimmune
  • CT adrenals (calcification in TB/haemorrhage)
  • MRI pituitary (secondary insufficiency)
💊 Treatment
  • Cortisol replacement: hydrocortisone 15–25 mg/day (split BD-TDS, mimicking diurnal rhythm)
  • Mineralocorticoid: fludrocortisone (primary Addison's only)
  • Sick day rules: double/triple dose during illness, surgery, or physiological stress
  • Adrenal crisis: IV hydrocortisone 100mg bolus + IV saline + glucose — emergency
  • Medical alert bracelet essential
  • Annual review: BP, electrolytes, weight, bone density

Hyperadrenalism
Adrenal
⚙️ Physiology & Pathophysiology
Three main patterns of adrenal excess: (1) Excess glucocorticoids → Cushing's Syndrome (see dedicated card). (2) Primary Hyperaldosteronism (Conn's syndrome): autonomous aldosterone from adrenal adenoma (60%) or bilateral hyperplasia → Na⁺ and water retention, K⁺ excretion → hypertension + hypokalaemia. (3) Phaeochromocytoma: catecholamine-secreting tumour of adrenal medulla (90%) or extra-adrenal paraganglia → episodic or sustained hypertension. Also: Congenital Adrenal Hyperplasia (CAH) — 21-hydroxylase deficiency blocks cortisol synthesis → ACTH ↑ → androgen excess.
🩺 Clinical Features
  • Conn's: hypertension (often resistant), hypokalaemia (weakness, cramps, polyuria), minimal oedema
  • Phaeochromocytoma: paroxysmal hypertension, headache, sweating, palpitations (classic triad), pallor
  • CAH (21-hydroxylase): virilisation in females, ambiguous genitalia at birth, salt-wasting crisis in neonates, precocious puberty
  • For glucocorticoid excess features — see Cushing's Disease card
🧪 Investigations
  • Conn's: aldosterone-to-renin ratio (ARR > 30) — screening; confirmatory salt loading test; CT adrenals; adrenal vein sampling (lateralise)
  • Phaeochromocytoma: 24h urinary metanephrines + catecholamines ↑; plasma metanephrines; CT/MRI adrenals; MIBG scan
  • CAH: 17-hydroxyprogesterone ↑; ACTH stimulation test; genetic testing
💊 Treatment
  • Conn's adenoma: laparoscopic adrenalectomy (curative)
  • Bilateral hyperplasia: spironolactone (mineralocorticoid antagonist) or eplerenone
  • Phaeochromocytoma: alpha-blockade FIRST (phenoxybenzamine), then beta-blockade, then laparoscopic adrenalectomy — order matters to prevent hypertensive crisis
  • CAH: glucocorticoid replacement (suppresses excess ACTH/androgen drive) + fludrocortisone if salt-wasting

Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia (MEN)
Neoplasia
⚙️ Physiology & Pathophysiology
MEN syndromes are hereditary autosomal dominant disorders causing multiple synchronous endocrine tumours. MEN1 (Wermer's): MEN1 tumour suppressor gene mutation → parathyroid hyperplasia/adenoma (95%), pancreatic NETs (50–70% — gastrinoma, insulinoma), pituitary adenoma (30–40% — prolactinoma most common). MEN2A (Sipple): RET proto-oncogene mutation → medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC, ~100%), phaeochromocytoma (50%), hyperparathyroidism (20%). MEN2B: RET mutation → MTC (~100%, aggressive early onset), phaeochromocytoma, marfanoid habitus, mucosal neuromas — NO parathyroid involvement.
🩺 Clinical Features
  • MEN1: recurrent kidney stones (hypercalcaemia), peptic ulcers (Zollinger-Ellison from gastrinoma), hypoglycaemia (insulinoma), visual changes/galactorrhoea (pituitary)
  • MEN2A: neck mass/goitre (MTC), episodic hypertension/sweating (phaeochromocytoma), kidney stones (hyperparathyroidism)
  • MEN2B: mucosal neuromas (lips, tongue), marfanoid body habitus, neck mass, adrenergic symptoms — youngest age of MTC onset
🧪 Investigations
  • Genetic testing: MEN1 gene / RET mutation (essential for family screening)
  • Calcium, PTH (hyperparathyroidism)
  • Fasting gastrin (gastrinoma), glucose/insulin ratio (insulinoma)
  • Serum calcitonin ↑ (MTC marker)
  • Urine/plasma metanephrines (phaeochromocytoma)
  • MRI pituitary, CT pancreas/chest/abdomen
💊 Treatment
  • MEN1: surveillance + treat each tumour as it arises; parathyroidectomy; PPIs for Zollinger-Ellison; resect symptomatic NETs
  • MEN2: prophylactic thyroidectomy — timing by risk: MEN2B in first 6 months of life; MEN2A by age 5
  • Always treat phaeochromocytoma BEFORE thyroid surgery
  • Genetic counselling and family screening for all first-degree relatives
  • Annual biochemical surveillance for mutation carriers

Thyroid Neoplasms
Neoplasia
⚙️ Pathophysiology & Classification
Thyroid neoplasms range from benign to highly malignant. Well-differentiated (follicular epithelium origin): Papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC, 80%) — BRAF V600E mutation, indolent, lymphatic spread, excellent prognosis (>95% 10-year survival). Follicular thyroid carcinoma (FTC, 10%) — RAS mutation, haematogenous spread (bone, lung), cannot distinguish from adenoma on FNA alone (requires capsular invasion on histology). Medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC, 5%) — arises from C-cells (calcitonin-secreting), sporadic or MEN2-associated, RET mutation. Anaplastic (<2%) — rapidly fatal, dedifferentiated, local invasion, median survival ~5 months. Benign: follicular adenoma — most common thyroid tumour overall.
🩺 Clinical Features
  • Painless thyroid nodule (most common presentation)
  • Neck mass — solid, hard, irregular (malignant features)
  • Hoarseness (recurrent laryngeal nerve invasion)
  • Dysphagia, dyspnoea (tracheal/oesophageal compression)
  • Cervical lymphadenopathy (PTC — lymphatic spread)
  • Bone pain, fractures (FTC — haematogenous mets)
  • Diarrhoea, flushing (MTC — calcitonin excess)
  • Rapid-growing hard mass (anaplastic — surgical emergency)
🧪 Investigations
  • Thyroid function tests (usually normal)
  • Serum calcitonin ↑ (MTC), CEA ↑ (MTC)
  • Thyroid ultrasound: TIRADS classification (size, echogenicity, margins, microcalcifications)
  • Fine Needle Aspiration Biopsy (FNAB): cytology — Bethesda classification (I–VI)
  • CT neck/chest: staging, tracheal involvement
  • Whole-body radioiodine scan (PTC/FTC post-thyroidectomy)
  • RET mutation testing (MTC — family screening)
  • BRAF mutation testing (PTC — prognostic)
💊 Treatment by Subtype
  • Benign nodule: ultrasound surveillance, repeat FNA if features change
  • PTC: total thyroidectomy + I-131 radioiodine ablation + TSH suppression (levothyroxine); excellent prognosis
  • FTC: total thyroidectomy + I-131 + TSH suppression
  • MTC: total thyroidectomy + prophylactic central neck dissection; I-131 NOT effective; vandetanib/cabozantinib for advanced disease
  • Anaplastic: rarely curative; palliative RT; lenvatinib/dabrafenib if BRAF+; clinical trials
  • Follow-up: thyroglobulin (PTC/FTC tumour marker), calcitonin (MTC), annual neck ultrasound
